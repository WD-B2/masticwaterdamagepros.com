Mold vs Mildew in Mastic, NY: Differences and Next Steps.

Mildew is a term for surface growth that is flat and powdery. Mold is the broader group. Here is how to tell them apart in practice.

By the Mastic Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 14, 2026.

(934) 223-1875.
Call Now.

People use mold and mildew as if they were separate things, but mildew is generally used for certain kinds of surface mold. In everyday use, mildew is flat, powdery and found on damp surfaces such as shower walls, while mold is the broader term for fungal growth.

This guide explains how they usually look, where each tends to appear, how cleaning differs and when a spot needs more than a sponge.

Mold and Mildew Side by Side.

Feature.Mildew.Mold.
Typical look.Flat, powdery, white or gray, sometimes yellow.Fuzzy or slimy, green, black, white or other colors.
Common places.Shower walls, window sills, damp fabrics.Walls, ceilings, wood, insulation, carpet.
Depth.Usually on the surface.Can grow into porous materials.
Cleaning.Often removed with detergent and a scrub.May require removal of materials.
What it signals.High humidity or poor ventilation.Ongoing moisture, possibly a leak.

Color alone is not reliable for identification.

Mastic local note.

The EPA advises drying water damaged materials within 24 to 48 hours to limit mold growth. The same moisture control applies to mildew.

The New York State Department of Labor states that its mold law applies to projects larger than 10 square feet and that it does not require a homeowner to clean up mold. The department also offers a search tool for licensed contractors.

Why the Difference Matters.

Surface growth on tile, glass or a sealed surface can usually be cleaned. Growth that has gone into drywall paper, wood or carpet may not come out, because the material holds the fungus.

Either one means moisture. The fix is the same: find the source and dry the area. Cleaning without fixing the moisture just delays the return.

Cleaning Small Mildew Spots.

1

Ventilate.

Open a window or run the exhaust fan.

2

Wipe.

Use detergent and water on hard surfaces, and wear gloves.

3

Dry.

Dry the surface fully with a clean cloth.

4

Reduce humidity.

Run the fan during showers and a dehumidifier in damp rooms.

Do not mix cleaning products, and do not brush dry growth. If you are unsure about a spot, photograph it and watch whether it returns.

When It Is More Than Mildew.

Spots keep returning.

The moisture source has not been fixed.

Soft or stained drywall.

Growth has gone into the material.

Large area.

New York law covers mold projects larger than 10 square feet.

Hidden smell.

A musty odor without visible growth suggests moisture behind a surface.

Prevention.

Use bathroom exhaust fans, fix leaks promptly and keep indoor humidity below about 60 percent. Clean spills and wet items quickly, and keep furniture a few inches off exterior walls to allow air movement.

In basements, a dehumidifier helps. Check it often and empty it or connect it to a drain.

Where Each Tends To Show Up.

Mildew shows up on shower walls, window sills, damp fabrics and leather left in humid rooms. It often starts at grout lines and corners where water sits. Mold shows up on drywall, wood, insulation and carpet after leaks and floods.

Both prefer dark, still, damp places. Opening curtains, running fans and wiping surfaces dry make a real difference. Fixing a dripping fixture matters more than any cleaner.

Choosing a Cleaner.

Mild detergent and water is often enough for hard surfaces. Some people use vinegar on small areas, and products labeled for mildew are available. Read labels, wear gloves and never combine cleaners.

No cleaner replaces moisture control. If the problem returns within weeks, there is a source you have not found, and it is time to look behind the surface or call someone to check.

Mildew on Fabric, Leather and Paper.

Mildew also appears on books, leather shoes, canvas and fabrics left in damp rooms. Wipe surfaces, dry items in sunlight and fresh air and store them in a dry space. Washable fabrics can be laundered according to their care labels.

Items that smell of mildew after cleaning may need more airing, or may not be recoverable. Do not store textiles in sealed plastic when they are damp.

Choosing Between Cleaning and Replacing.

Hard, non-porous surfaces such as tile, glass and sealed metal can usually be cleaned. Porous materials such as drywall, ceiling tile and insulation are harder to clean once they are affected, and removing them is often the better path.

Wood sits in between. Surface growth on solid framing can often be cleaned, while deeply affected or soft wood may need replacing. A licensed professional can look at the material and explain which approach fits.

Whatever you choose, fix the moisture source first. Cleaning without fixing the cause invites the same problem back.

What To Do While You Decide.

  • Photograph what you see before anything is moved or cleaned.
  • Fix or shut off the water source if you can do it safely.
  • Do not touch electrical items while standing in water.
  • Write down dates, readings and who you spoke with.
  • Call the line if you want a second opinion on the next step.

Standards and Sources.

The ANSI/IICRC S520 is the published standard for professional mold remediation, and New York Labor Law Article 32 sets the licensing rules for projects over 10 square feet. We cite both as references for mold vs mildew. They describe how the work is planned and who may do it, and they are not a claim about any certification.
