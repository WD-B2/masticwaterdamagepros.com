Homeowners Insurance and Water Damage in Mastic, NY.

Coverage turns on where the water came from and how fast it arrived. This guide explains the common categories in plain language.

By the Mastic Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 14, 2026.

Homeowners policies cover some water damage and exclude other kinds, and the dividing line is usually the source of the water. A burst pipe and a flooded yard can leave the same stain on a floor, yet the policy may treat them very differently.

This guide explains the common categories, what New York regulators say about flood and back-up coverage, and what to document. It is general information. Your policy wording and your insurer decide coverage, and neither we nor this page can tell you what yours will pay.

Sudden and Accidental Versus Gradual.

Many policies cover water damage that is sudden and accidental, such as a supply line that bursts or a water heater that fails. The same policies often exclude damage that happens slowly over time, such as a drip under a sink that rots a cabinet over several months.

The reason is that insurers treat slow leaks as a maintenance issue. If a leak is found and fixed quickly, the claim is stronger. If water has been seeping for months, the insurer may say the damage was preventable.

Policy language varies, so read the section on water damage and the list of exclusions. Look for words such as continuous, repeated seepage and sudden.

Mastic local note.

The New York State Department of Financial Services states that standard homeowners policies do not cover flood, which requires separate flood insurance such as NFIP coverage. It adds that water back-up and sewage coverage is typically an endorsement and is usually excluded when flooding caused the back-up.

Flood and Back-Up Are Separate.

People are often surprised to learn that flood damage is not part of a standard homeowners policy. Flood coverage is sold separately, often through the National Flood Insurance Program. Water back-up through drains and sump pumps is another optional piece.

If you live near a bay, creek or river, ask your agent what flood coverage you have and what it excludes. Flood policies often have a waiting period, so they cannot be bought after a storm is on the way.

What To Do Before You Call the Insurer.

  1. Stop the source of water if you can do so safely.
  2. Photograph and video the damage before anything is moved.
  3. Take steps to prevent more damage, such as extraction and drying, and keep receipts.
  4. Keep damaged items until the adjuster has seen them, unless they are unsafe.
  5. Write down the date and time you discovered the loss, and what you did.

Mitigation is usually expected. Policies commonly require you to protect the property from further damage, and the cost of reasonable mitigation is often part of the claim.

When a Claim Is Denied.

A denial should give a reason tied to the policy language. If the reason is unclear, ask for it in writing and read the exact wording. You can ask the insurer to review the decision and provide the evidence they relied on.

The New York Department of Financial Services provides consumer information on how to dispute a claim. A restoration company can provide moisture logs and photographs, but it cannot decide coverage or promise approval.

Reading the Exclusions Section.

Most of the confusion comes from the exclusions page. It lists causes the policy will not pay for, and it often uses terms such as surface water, seepage, wear and tear and neglect. Each term has a meaning in the policy definitions.

Read the definitions, not just the headings. If a word is unclear, ask your agent to explain it in writing and keep the answer with your policy. A short email today can save a long argument after a loss.
